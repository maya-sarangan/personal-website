You're shoved into a two by two room by the royal guards. Their faces are emotionless as they silently lock the door and close the ceiling. This is the punishment for your crime. Sitting is impossible - so you stand, ignoring the ache in your feet. This is your death sentence. You take a while to look around, observe your surroundings. You realize there are holes for oxygen to come through, but where is the slot for food and water? You decide to just wait for a few minutes. But those minutes turn into hours, and soon it is nighttime. That's when it dawns on you, no one is coming to give you food and water. You tell yourself you'll survive, but you know that isn't true. Soon, days turn into weeks and by then, you're dying of thirst and starvation. Hunger rips apart your insides and your throat is so dry you cannot even scream. Soon you collapse and die. This was a real punishment in the Mughal Empire. People would die of thirst and starvation over the course of a few weeks. Now imagine, the small room is an entire country. Instead of one prisoner, there are millions of women waiting for a slow death. That is the story of [Osama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osama_(film)).

Many Afghanistan women were held captive in their own country because of religious fundamentalism. They had to wear a [burqa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burqa), covering their entire body, leaving only a few, small holes for sight. They could not leave the house without a male companion, or else the Taliban would arrest them. If they wore their burqa incorrectly, the Taliban would arrest them. If they were caught singing, the Taliban would arrest them. If there is no man in the family, they are prisoners in their own home.  This is the story of a young girl living in Kabul, Afghanistan. All the males in her family have died in wars, so she only lives with her mother and grandmother. They cannot even leave the house for food and water, what will they do?

Hundreds of women march down the streets. Hundreds of nameless, faceless women covered by a blue burqa. Hundreds of women fighting for a job, fighting for their _identity_. Their chanting voices slice through the afternoon silence like a sharp knife. They hold up signs and protest, saying that they need jobs even though they're women. The young girl and her mother watch this from the sidewalk. They make sure to stay hidden, so nobody notices their traveling without a male companion. The young girl worries the Taliban will come and arrest them, so she urges her mother to take them home. Her fears become reality as the Taliban bring trucks and start pouring heavy torrents of water on the protesters, forcing them to go home. The water drenches the girl's burqa  and she flees home with her mother, locking the door behind them. The Taliban even come to the hospital, where her mother works as nurse, and arrests a foreign female doctor who is treating patients. The mother doesn't even get paid anymore since the hospital is in bad shape, so they leave for home.

As the girl falls asleep in her grandmother's lap, she hears her mother's cries as she weeps for the death of her husband and brother in the Kabul and Russian wars. The girl's grandmother realizes they will starve to death if they don't get food soon. She decides to cut the girl's hair, in hopes that it will make her appear as a boy. The girl protests, but her grandmother insists. She tells the story of a man who turned to a woman by passing through a rainbow. Just like that, as the girl wakes up in the morning, her hair is cut and now she is a boy. She puts her hair in a pot of soil, like a child, and hopes it will grow back. Her mother requests an old friend of her husband to employ her daughter in the disguise of a boy. Next day, she starts working in the tea shop.

Soon, Taliban forces everyone to attend the prayer during work. The owner guides her to the mosque and tells her to pray. She doesn't know how the boys would pray. The owner realizes this and quickly teaches her how to properly pray like men. The government doesn't even care if families do not have proper food, water, and education. It only cares if they go to prayers every day.  Then, the Taliban collects all boys and forces them to religious and military studies. The girl is also forced to join them. She is forced to learn the boys' way of showering. She is forced to play with other boys. What happens to her at the end? That forms to remaining part of the 2003 Afghan movie - 'Osama'.

In one of the scenes, people gather to celebrate a wedding. At the wedding, there is no groom, there is only a picture of him since he is at war. They celebrate the wedding but the Taliban comes hearing their singing. Singing is not allowed by Taliban. They all quickly pull down their burqas and start wailing, pretending they are at a funeral and crying. The Taliban believes them and leaves.  It is ironic that they accept a funeral and crying is normal while singing and happiness raises suspicion.

Another notable scene is where she draws an image of a young girl with long hair, in the window of the tea shop. Through that image she sees the Taliban moving across the city. Physically, she has become a boy. In terms of responsibility, she is playing the role of an adult. But, she is a child. She is seeing the world, seeing the suffering, seeing the brutality of a regime through the eyes of that young child with long hair.

In the entire movie, the girl and almost every woman remain nameless. They do not have a life, a name or an identity. Even the name 'Osama' was given to her by another young boy.  

At the end, she is forced to marry an old mullah with 3 wives. She was a child. She put her hair in soil, hoping it would grow. She drew on windows. She fell asleep in her grandmother's lap, listening to her tell stories about magical rainbows. She was just a child, but she was forced to marry a much older man.

This movie shows the harsh reality of what many women in Afghanistan must go through every day. Or, to varying degrees, women all over the World......
